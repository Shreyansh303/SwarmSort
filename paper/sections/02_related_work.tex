\section{Related Work and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews vision-based waste recognition, waste data from India and South Asia, and hyperparameter optimization with swarm methods, and then derives the research gaps that SwarmSort addresses. The comparison asks not which method is most accurate but what each study tested, how it chose hyperparameters and how it reported uncertainty.

\subsection{Review Method}
\label{subsec:review-method}

We gathered the literature in October 2026 by keyword search and citation tracing, using Crossref, arXiv, Europe PMC and PubMed Central, OpenAlex and publisher pages. The search started from six seed papers: a waste-detection pipeline~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, the TrashNet report~\cite{yang2016classification}, two TrashNet classifiers~\cite{kaya2023classification,sayed2024intelligent}, a smart-bin prototype~\cite{alshammari2025smart}$^{\dagger}$ and a PSO-tuned convolutional neural network (CNN) from medical imaging~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn}. We then traced references and searched for reviews of artificial intelligence (AI) in waste management, waste and litter datasets, waste detectors, work from India and South Asia, and metaheuristic or hyperparameter optimization (HPO) for CNNs.

We included vision work on waste or litter (classification, detection, segmentation, datasets and reviews) and metaheuristic or HPO work for CNNs, preferring peer-reviewed work from 2016 to 2026; classic method papers are older. Preprints, technical reports and workshop papers were kept where they fill a gap, and their status is named. Each reference was confirmed to exist through Crossref, arXiv or the publisher's or authors' page, and we recorded whether we read its full text, only its abstract or only its bibliographic record. Waste-vision works available to us only at the latter two levels are marked $^{\dagger}$ and are cited only for their existence and stated scope; method and software references are cited for what they introduce. This is a targeted review, not a systematic one, so every statement below about ``the literature'' means the literature we reviewed.

\subsection{Vision-Based Waste Recognition: From Classification to Detection}
\label{subsec:rw-waste}

Much of the vision work on waste sorting classifies images of single objects. TrashNet, released with a Stanford course report by Yang and Thung~\cite{yang2016classification}, photographed one item at a time on a white posterboard; its public version has 2,527 images in six classes. In the report, a support vector machine (SVM) on SIFT features reached 63\% test accuracy and a CNN trained from scratch only 22\%, which the authors blamed on unsuitable hyperparameters; they listed a more thorough hyperparameter search and multi-object detection as future work. Kaya~\cite{kaya2023classification} truncates pretrained Xception and InceptionResNetV2 networks, reaches 89.72\% test accuracy with one fixed training setting for all models, and names TrashNet's plain backgrounds as a limitation. Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent} classify the same images with a metaheuristic-tuned InceptionV3 (Section~\ref{subsec:rw-hpo}). The smart-bin prototype of Alshammari et al.~\cite{alshammari2025smart}$^{\dagger}$ routes items to bins with a MobileNetV3-based classifier on a Raspberry Pi, and TrashBox~\cite{kumsetty2022trashbox}$^{\dagger}$ is presented as a classification set of 17,785 images in seven classes, including medical waste and e-waste, despite ``detection'' in its title. Each image receives one label, which suits a bin with a single-item chute but not a heap, a conveyor or a street.

Reviews point the same way. Abdallah et al.~\cite{abdallah2020artificial}$^{\dagger}$ survey 85 studies of AI in solid waste management from 2004 to 2019, covering forecasting of waste characteristics, bin-level detection, process-parameter prediction, vehicle routing and planning. Lu and Chen~\cite{lu2022computer}$^{\dagger}$ find many computer-vision studies of waste sorting preliminary, set in simplified environments and built on artificially collected data, and call for work on real-world complexity and for open datasets. Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} find that most vision studies they cover classify whole images by material on their own image sets, that detection studies are fewer and report lower accuracy, and that simple lab pictures differ from real waste. The identification accuracies they collect, 72.8\% to 99.95\%, mix classification and detection, so the range cannot rank methods.

Detection and segmentation datasets move the problem into cluttered scenes, but each covers one setting. TACO~\cite{proenca2020taco}$^{\dagger}$, a crowd-sourced dataset released as a preprint, offered 1,500 photographs of litter in the wild with 4,784 annotations at release. ZeroWaste~\cite{bashkirova2022zerowaste}$^{\dagger}$ records deformable, translucent objects in heavy clutter on a recycling-facility sorting line, and WaRP~\cite{yudin2024hierarchical}$^{\dagger}$ targets hierarchical waste detection in images from recycling plants. UAVVaste~\cite{kraft2021autonomous} has 772 low-altitude drone images with 3,716 annotations; its authors note that TACO's near-ground, large objects make it a poor fit for aerial detection. PlastOPol~\cite{cordova2022litter} has 2,418 crowd-sourced urban, beach and forest images with 5,300 annotations of one ``litter'' class. The TrashCan technical report~\cite{hong2020trashcan} labels 7,212 deep-sea ROV images with boxes and masks, FloW~\cite{cheng2021flow}$^{\dagger}$ covers floating waste in inland waters, partly paired with radar data, and MJU-Waste~\cite{wang2020multilevel} has 2,475 RGB-D laboratory images of people holding waste items, with one ``waste'' class. The strand is real but fragmented: aerial, underwater, conveyor, hand-held and street settings each have their own data, often with one generic class and with class schemes that do not match.

Detector studies build on these resources. Majchrowska et al.~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, the pipeline closest to ours and the starting point of this work, merge public datasets into a one-class ``detect-waste'' set and a seven-category ``classify-waste'' set that follows the sorting rules of Gda\'{n}sk. Because direct multi-class detection performed poorly, they localize litter with EfficientDet-D2 and classify each crop with EfficientNet-B2. Their hyperparameters were set by hand, with no search procedure described, and single-run results are reported per source dataset on a held-out 20\% described as a validation and testing set. Fulton et al.~\cite{fulton2019robotic} compare YOLOv2, Tiny-YOLO, Faster R-CNN and SSD for underwater litter on a GPU, a Jetson TX2 and a CPU, with settings suggested by the community. Melinte et al.~\cite{melinte2020deep} add boxes to TrashNet and report 97.63\% precision with SSD on single-object studio images. Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2021novel} train YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-tiny on 6,437 self-collected images in six classes and report 94.99\% and 51.95\% mAP on a split used for both validation and testing. Liao and Juang~\cite{liao2022realtime} run YOLOv4-Tiny-3l at 22 frames per second on an Xavier NX for drone-based coastal litter monitoring. In a workshop paper, Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning} train five detectors with privileged information on one drone dataset and test them on two others, and S\'{a}nchez-Ferrer et al.~\cite{sanchezferrer2023experimental}$^{\dagger}$ study Mask R-CNN for marine debris under data scarcity, adding synthetic scenes. In these papers the detector and the data are the subject of study; hyperparameters are a setup detail.

SwarmSort's detector belongs to the single-stage family introduced by YOLO~\cite{redmon2016you}. Single-stage models recur in the speed-oriented studies above: YOLOv3~\cite{kumar2021novel}, YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 on embedded boards~\cite{kraft2021autonomous}, YOLOv4-Tiny-3l~\cite{liao2022realtime} and YOLOv5~\cite{cordova2022litter}; in the PlastOPol study, YOLOv5x was the most accurate of seven detectors and YOLOv5s ran at up to 5.19 frames per second on a phone. We use the Ultralytics YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023ultralytics} with COCO-pretrained weights~\cite{lin2014microsoft}, a nano model suited to a smart bin or a phone.

\subsection{Waste Data From India and South Asia}
\label{subsec:rw-india}

Waste image data from India are scattered and mostly not built for detection. The earliest work we found, SpotGarbage~\cite{mittal2016spotgarbage}$^{\dagger}$ from IIT Ropar, is a smartphone app that detects and coarsely segments garbage regions in geo-tagged photos for citizen reporting, a binary garbage-or-not task. Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2021novel} frame their YOLOv3 study around Indian waste statistics, but their images are mostly clean, with one or a few items each, the collection location is not stated, and no separate test split is used. AquaVision~\cite{panwar2020aquavision}$^{\dagger}$, by mainly Indian authors, detects waste in water bodies with a subset derived from TACO rather than an Indian collection, and TrashBox~\cite{kumsetty2022trashbox}$^{\dagger}$, from NIT Karnataka, is a classification set.

The resource closest to an Indian street benchmark is DWSD~\cite{ali2025dwsd}: 784 phone-camera images taken around Jadavpur and Garia in Kolkata and in Berhampore, West Bengal, showing streets, parks, lawns and garbage areas, with pixel masks for 14 waste classes. It is a segmentation dataset: its baselines report pixel accuracy only (89.39\% for DeepLabv3+), it gives no detection results, and the paper is internally inconsistent about the dataset's name and image counts. Elsewhere in South Asia, UGV-NBWASTE~\cite{isalm2025ugvnbwaste} provides 3,600 images of eight non-biodegradable waste types from Barisal, Bangladesh, with oriented boxes, but only about 1.1 objects per image and no baseline results, and the pLitterStreet preprint~\cite{mandhati2024plitterstreet}$^{\dagger}$ detects and maps street-level plastic litter from vehicle-mounted cameras, with images from Thailand, Vietnam and Sri Lanka. Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} list data scarcity in developing countries among the limits of the field. In the literature we reviewed, no Indian real-world waste test set came with published object-detection baselines.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization and Swarm Intelligence}
\label{subsec:rw-hpo}

Particle swarm optimization (PSO), introduced by Kennedy and Eberhart~\cite{kennedy1995particle}, moves a population of candidate solutions (particles) through the search space; each particle's velocity combines its previous motion with pulls toward its own best position and the swarm's best position. Shi and Eberhart~\cite{shi1998modified} introduced the inertia weight, a factor on the previous velocity, and Clerc and Kennedy~\cite{clerc2002particle} analyzed a particle's trajectory to derive coefficients that control convergence and keep the swarm from diverging, the basis of constriction-factor PSO. PSO needs no gradients and evaluates a fixed number of candidates per round, which suits problems where every evaluation is a network training. Our settings, including the inertia schedule, are given in Section~\ref{subsec:pso}.

Random search~\cite{bergstra2012random} is the natural reference for any guided search: it samples settings independently, needs nothing beyond the search space and parallelizes without coordination, so an optimizer that cannot beat it at the same number of evaluations has not shown that its guidance helps. Multi-fidelity methods instead cut the cost of each evaluation: Hyperband~\cite{li2018hyperband} speeds up random search by giving more training resources to promising configurations and stopping weak ones early. SwarmSort uses a simpler, fixed form of low fidelity, a short proxy training whose ranking we checked against longer training before the \genone search (Section~\ref{subsec:proxy}), and Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent} likewise score candidates with 15-epoch trainings.

Metaheuristic tuning of CNNs appears in several domains, but the evidence we found is thin. Aguerchi et al.~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn} use PSO to choose three architecture hyperparameters (number of filters, kernel size and stride) of a small CNN that classifies mammogram masses, with the training hyperparameters fixed. The swarm has 4 particles and runs 3 iterations, about 12 network evaluations, and the authors run no baseline of their own: no default setting, no random search and no other optimizer. They report a single result and note that a metaheuristic needs repeated runs and statistical analysis. Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent} give the most complete comparison we found in the waste domain. A multi-objective beluga whale optimizer (MBWO) tunes the learning rate, batch size and dropout period of InceptionV3 on TrashNet (population 30, 20 iterations, 15 training epochs per evaluation). Four other metaheuristics run with the same population, iterations, bounds and fitness; an untuned model gives a before-and-after reference (90.76\% accuracy); and a Wilcoxon rank-sum test on the best search fitness over independent runs finds MBWO significantly better than three competitors but not than the fourth (EO). The number of runs is not stated, random search is absent, the test metrics are single numbers without intervals, and the headline accuracy differs between the abstract (97.75\%) and the results table (97.57\%). Mao et al.~\cite{mao2021recycling}$^{\dagger}$ apply a genetic algorithm to a CNN that classifies recycling waste. All three tune classifiers, and neither of the two we read in full compares against random search.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis and Research Gaps}
\label{subsec:gaps}

Table~\ref{tab:lit-matrix} compares 15 studies read in full on the points that decide whether a tuning claim can be trusted, from how hyperparameters were chosen to how uncertainty was reported and whether testing crossed domains. Three patterns stand out. Only two of the 15 tune hyperparameters with a metaheuristic, both for classification, and only one of those compares tuning methods. None reports a confidence interval on a test metric. Only Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning} test on public datasets unseen in training; the others test on shifted footage~\cite{fulton2019robotic}, a lab-made set~\cite{melinte2020deep} or new sites described only qualitatively~\cite{liao2022realtime}, evaluate two datasets separately~\cite{cordova2022litter}, or stay within one dataset. The four gaps below (G1--G4) follow from these patterns, each stated with the evidence against it.

\textbf{Gap 1 (task and scene).} The classification studies of Section~\ref{subsec:rw-waste}~\cite{yang2016classification,kaya2023classification,sayed2024intelligent} and~\cite{alshammari2025smart,kumsetty2022trashbox}$^{\dagger}$ assign one label per image, and the TrashNet-based ones photograph one object on a plain background; Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} find whole-image classification the most common approach in the vision work they survey. Even some detection studies rest on near-single-object data~\cite{melinte2020deep,kumar2021novel,isalm2025ugvnbwaste} (Sections~\ref{subsec:rw-waste} and~\ref{subsec:rw-india}). The counter-evidence is substantial: multi-object waste detection is an established line of work, largely in computer-vision, robotics and sensing venues, with TACO, ZeroWaste, FloW and WaRP~\cite{proenca2020taco,bashkirova2022zerowaste,cheng2021flow,yudin2024hierarchical}$^{\dagger}$, UAVVaste, PlastOPol and TrashCan~\cite{kraft2021autonomous,cordova2022litter,hong2020trashcan}, and the pipeline of Majchrowska et al.~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}. Because our review is not systematic, we do not estimate how much smaller that strand is. The gap is therefore worded as follows: \emph{in the literature we reviewed, waste recognition for sorting is dominated by single-label classification of one object on a plain background; multi-object detection in cluttered scenes is a smaller strand, and several detection studies still rely on single-object studio images.}

\textbf{Gap 2 (optimization).} Metaheuristic tuning in waste vision targets classifiers~\cite{sayed2024intelligent},~\cite{mao2021recycling}$^{\dagger}$, and PSO tuning of CNNs appears in other domains, for three architecture hyperparameters in the mammography study of Aguerchi et al.~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn}. Every waste detection or segmentation model study we read in full used default, published or hand-chosen training hyperparameters (in one case aided by a learning-rate range test~\cite{melinte2020deep}) or a tuning step it does not describe; the only search described is a grid search over the post-processing parameters of MJU-Waste~\cite{wang2020multilevel} (Table~\ref{tab:lit-matrix}). In the sections we read, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} mention PSO and genetic algorithms only for routing and prediction. Against this, metaheuristic CNN tuning does exist, so the gap concerns only its combination with waste detectors; two detection papers mention tuning without describing it~\cite{liao2022realtime,bartolo2025learning}, so a search there cannot be ruled out; and we could not check the training setup of detection works read only as abstracts. The gap is worded as follows: \emph{in the literature we reviewed, no waste detector had its training hyperparameters tuned by a swarm optimizer; detection studies used default or hand-set values, or a tuning step they do not describe.}

\textbf{Gap 3 (evidence).} Where tuning is evaluated, the baselines are weak: Aguerchi et al.~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn} run none, and Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent} compare only against other metaheuristics and report test metrics as single numbers (Section~\ref{subsec:rw-hpo}). None of the detection and segmentation papers read in full reports variation across training runs, confidence intervals or significance tests on its accuracy; Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning} state that statistical analysis was not applicable, and Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2021novel} report the best value on the split that also serves as their test set. Without a random-search arm at the same budget, a gain cannot be credited to the optimizer's guidance rather than to the search effort itself. The counter-evidence matters: Sayed et al. do compare optimizers at a stated equal population and iteration count, include an untuned reference and test significance on the search fitness, so we do not claim that no study compares optimizers or tests significance. The gap is worded as follows: \emph{metaheuristic tuning in this area is compared against other metaheuristics or against nothing; we found no comparison with random search~\cite{bergstra2012random} at an equal evaluation budget, and test results are reported as single numbers without confidence intervals.} SwarmSort narrows this gap without closing it: with one training seed per arm and one search seed per method, its intervals cover test-set sampling only (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

\textbf{Gap 4 (generalization).} The TrashNet-based classifiers never leave the studio, and two reviews point to simplified, lab-like data~\cite{lu2022computer}$^{\dagger}$,~\cite{fang2023artificial}. The dataset matters: in the PlastOPol study the same YOLOv5x, trained and tested on each dataset separately, scores 84.9 AP$_{50}$ on PlastOPol but 63.3 on TACO~\cite{cordova2022litter}, so a result on one dataset says little about another. Degradation at new sites is reported only qualitatively~\cite{liao2022realtime}, and Majchrowska et al.~\cite{majchrowska2022deep} report in-distribution results per source dataset. The Indian work of Section~\ref{subsec:rw-india} offers segmentation pixel accuracy~\cite{ali2025dwsd}, mostly clean images without a separate test split~\cite{kumar2021novel}, TACO-derived data~\cite{panwar2020aquavision}$^{\dagger}$ and garbage-region segmentation~\cite{mittal2016spotgarbage}$^{\dagger}$, but no detection benchmark. Against this, cross-domain tests do exist: Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning}, in a recent workshop paper, test on datasets unseen in training, Fulton et al.~\cite{fulton2019robotic} test on footage shifted in time and place, and S\'{a}nchez-Ferrer et al.~\cite{sanchezferrer2023experimental}$^{\dagger}$ add synthetic scenes to scarce real training data. South Asian detection data also exist in UGV-NBWASTE~\cite{isalm2025ugvnbwaste}, which reports no baselines, and in the pLitterStreet preprint~\cite{mandhati2024plitterstreet}$^{\dagger}$. The gap is worded as follows: \emph{studio-to-real-world and cross-dataset evaluation is rare and recent, and we found no Indian real-world waste test set with published object-detection baselines.} SwarmSort reports detection results on DWSD, where every arm scores only 0.023--0.027 \mapfifty (Section~\ref{subsec:res-domain}). Because our DWSD boxes come from mask regions that merge touching items, and the class mapping was inferred from the masks, these numbers measure a labeling mismatch as well as the model.

\textbf{Observation O5 (reporting of positive results).} The applied papers we read present their proposed methods as improvements; Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent}, for example, call their optimizer the best although its fitness advantage over one competitor was not significant. Partial negative findings do appear: a CNN far below an SVM~\cite{yang2016classification}, direct multi-class detection set aside because it performed poorly~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, synthetic data that relieve data scarcity only to a limited extent~\cite{sanchezferrer2023experimental}$^{\dagger}$, and weaker performance on shifted data~\cite{fulton2019robotic,liao2022realtime}. We found no paper reporting that a tuning method failed to beat a simple baseline; as evidence of absence from a targeted sample, this is an observation, not a gap. SwarmSort reports and analyzes its null and negative results: the \genone tie, no significant difference between PSO and random search on the mixed, studio and real-world test sets of \gentwo, no significant tuning gain on real-world photographs, and the failure of all arms on Indian photographs. Table~\ref{tab:gap-map} maps each gap and the observation to the part of SwarmSort that addresses it and the section that reports it.
