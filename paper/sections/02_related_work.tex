\section{Related Work and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews vision-based waste recognition, waste data from India and South Asia, and hyperparameter optimization, and then derives the research gaps.

\subsection{Review Method}
\label{subsec:review-method}

In October 2026 we searched Crossref, arXiv, Europe PMC, PubMed Central, OpenAlex and publisher pages by keyword (waste reviews, datasets and detectors; India and South Asia; metaheuristic and hyperparameter optimization) and by citation tracing from six seed papers, all discussed below~\cite{majchrowska2022deep,yang2016classification,kaya2023classification,sayed2024intelligent,aguerchi2024cnn},~\cite{alshammari2025smart}$^{\dagger}$. We included vision work on waste or litter and optimization work on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), preferring peer-reviewed papers from 2016 to 2026 (classic method papers are older) and naming the status of preprints, technical reports and workshop papers kept to fill a gap. We confirmed that each reference exists (via Crossref, arXiv or publisher pages) and recorded whether we read its full text, only its abstract or only its record. Waste-vision works known to us only at the latter two levels are marked $^{\dagger}$ and cited only for their existence and stated scope; method papers are cited for what they introduce. This is a targeted review, not a systematic one, so ``the literature'' below means the literature we reviewed.

\subsection{Vision-Based Waste Recognition: From Classification to Detection}
\label{subsec:rw-waste}

Much waste-sorting vision work classifies images of single objects. TrashNet, released with a Stanford course report by Yang and Thung~\cite{yang2016classification}, photographed one item at a time on a white posterboard (2,527 public images, six classes). In the report, a support vector machine (SVM) on SIFT features reached 63\% test accuracy and a CNN trained from scratch only 22\%, which the authors blamed on unsuitable hyperparameters, leaving a more thorough hyperparameter search and multi-object detection to future work. Kaya~\cite{kaya2023classification} truncates pretrained Xception and InceptionResNetV2 networks, reaching 89.72\% test accuracy with one fixed training setting, and names TrashNet's plain backgrounds as a limitation; Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent} use a metaheuristic-tuned InceptionV3 (Section~\ref{subsec:rw-hpo}). The smart bin of Alshammari et al.~\cite{alshammari2025smart}$^{\dagger}$ routes items with a MobileNetV3-based classifier on a Raspberry Pi, and TrashBox~\cite{kumsetty2022trashbox}$^{\dagger}$ is presented as a classification set of 17,785 images in seven classes despite ``detection'' in its title. One label per image suits a single-item chute but not a heap, a conveyor or a street.

Reviews point the same way. Abdallah et al.~\cite{abdallah2020artificial}$^{\dagger}$ survey 85 studies of artificial intelligence in solid waste management from 2004 to 2019. Lu and Chen~\cite{lu2022computer}$^{\dagger}$ find many waste-sorting vision studies preliminary, set in simplified environments and built on artificially collected data, and call for real-world complexity and open datasets. Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} find that most vision studies they cover classify whole images by material, that detection studies are fewer and less accurate, and that lab pictures differ from real waste; their collected accuracies (72.8\% to 99.95\%) mix classification and detection, so they cannot rank methods.

Detection and segmentation datasets move the problem into cluttered scenes. TACO~\cite{proenca2020taco}$^{\dagger}$, a crowd-sourced preprint dataset, offered 1,500 photographs of litter in the wild with 4,784 annotations at release. ZeroWaste~\cite{bashkirova2022zerowaste}$^{\dagger}$ records deformable, translucent objects in heavy clutter on a recycling-facility sorting line, and WaRP~\cite{yudin2024hierarchical}$^{\dagger}$ targets hierarchical waste detection in recycling-plant images. UAVVaste~\cite{kraft2021autonomous} has 772 low-altitude drone images with 3,716 annotations; its authors find TACO's large, near-ground objects a poor fit for aerial detection. PlastOPol~\cite{cordova2022litter} has 2,418 crowd-sourced urban, beach and forest images with 5,300 annotations of one ``litter'' class. The TrashCan technical report~\cite{hong2020trashcan} labels 7,212 deep-sea ROV images with boxes and masks, FloW~\cite{cheng2021flow}$^{\dagger}$ covers floating waste in inland waters, partly with radar data, and MJU-Waste~\cite{wang2020multilevel} has 2,475 RGB-D lab images of people holding waste, in one ``waste'' class. Aerial, underwater, conveyor, hand-held and street settings thus each have their own data, often with one generic class and mismatched class schemes.

Majchrowska et al.~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, the pipeline closest to ours and the starting point of this work, merge public datasets into a one-class ``detect-waste'' set and a seven-category ``classify-waste'' set that follows the sorting rules of Gda\'{n}sk. As direct multi-class detection performed poorly, they localize litter with EfficientDet-D2 and classify each crop with EfficientNet-B2, with hand-set hyperparameters and single-run results per source dataset on a held-out 20\% described as a validation and testing set. Fulton et al.~\cite{fulton2019robotic} compare YOLOv2, Tiny-YOLO, Faster R-CNN and SSD for underwater litter on desktop and embedded hardware. Melinte et al.~\cite{melinte2020deep} add boxes to TrashNet and report 97.63\% precision with SSD. Liao and Juang~\cite{liao2022realtime} run YOLOv4-Tiny-3l at 22 frames per second on an Xavier NX for drone-based coastal litter monitoring. In a workshop paper, Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning} train five detectors with privileged information on one drone dataset and test them on two others, and S\'{a}nchez-Ferrer et al.~\cite{sanchezferrer2023experimental}$^{\dagger}$ study Mask R-CNN for marine debris under data scarcity, adding synthetic scenes. Hyperparameters are a setup detail in all of these (Table~\ref{tab:lit-matrix}).

SwarmSort's detector belongs to the single-stage family introduced by YOLO~\cite{redmon2016you}, which recurs in speed-oriented waste studies~\cite{kumar2021novel,kraft2021autonomous,liao2022realtime,cordova2022litter}; in the PlastOPol study, YOLOv5x was the most accurate of seven detectors and YOLOv5s ran at up to 5.19 frames per second on a phone. We use YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023ultralytics} with COCO-pretrained weights~\cite{lin2014microsoft} (Section~\ref{subsec:detector}).

\subsection{Waste Data From India and South Asia}
\label{subsec:rw-india}

Waste image data from India are scattered and mostly not built for detection. The earliest we found, SpotGarbage~\cite{mittal2016spotgarbage}$^{\dagger}$ from IIT Ropar, is a smartphone app that detects and coarsely segments garbage in geo-tagged photos for citizen reporting. Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2021novel} frame their YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-tiny study (6,437 self-collected images in six classes; 94.99\% and 51.95\% mAP on a split used for both validation and testing) around Indian waste statistics, but the images are mostly clean, with one or a few items each, and their location is not stated. AquaVision~\cite{panwar2020aquavision}$^{\dagger}$, by mainly Indian authors, detects waste in water bodies with a TACO-derived subset, and TrashBox~\cite{kumsetty2022trashbox}$^{\dagger}$, from NIT Karnataka, is a classification set.

The resource closest to an Indian street benchmark is DWSD~\cite{ali2025dwsd}: 784 phone-camera images of streets, parks, lawns and garbage areas around Kolkata (Jadavpur, Garia) and Berhampore, West Bengal, with pixel masks for 14 waste classes. Its baselines report pixel accuracy only (89.39\% for DeepLabv3+), with no detection results, and the paper is internally inconsistent about the dataset's name and image counts. Elsewhere in South Asia, UGV-NBWASTE~\cite{isalm2025ugvnbwaste} provides 3,600 images of eight non-biodegradable waste types from Barisal, Bangladesh, with oriented boxes but only about 1.1 objects per image and no baselines, and the pLitterStreet preprint~\cite{mandhati2024plitterstreet}$^{\dagger}$ maps street-level plastic litter from vehicle-mounted cameras in Thailand, Vietnam and Sri Lanka. Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} list data scarcity in developing countries among the field's limits.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization and Swarm Intelligence}
\label{subsec:rw-hpo}

Particle swarm optimization (PSO)~\cite{kennedy1995particle} moves a population of candidate solutions (particles) through the search space; each particle's velocity combines its previous motion with pulls toward its own and the swarm's best positions. Shi and Eberhart~\cite{shi1998modified} introduced the inertia weight, a factor on the previous velocity, and Clerc and Kennedy~\cite{clerc2002particle} derived convergence-controlling coefficients from particle trajectories, the basis of constriction-factor PSO. PSO needs no gradients and evaluates a fixed number of candidates per round, which suits costly evaluations such as network trainings; our settings are in Section~\ref{subsec:pso}.

Random search~\cite{bergstra2012random} is the natural reference for any guided search: it samples settings independently and parallelizes freely, so an optimizer that cannot beat it at the same number of evaluations has not shown that its guidance helps. Multi-fidelity methods instead cut the cost of each evaluation; Hyperband~\cite{li2018hyperband} speeds up random search by giving more resources to promising configurations and stopping weak ones early. SwarmSort uses a simpler, fixed low fidelity: a short proxy training (Section~\ref{subsec:proxy}).

Metaheuristic tuning of CNNs appears in several domains, but the evidence we found is thin. Aguerchi et al.~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn} use PSO to choose three architecture hyperparameters (filters, kernel size and stride) of a small CNN for mammogram masses, with training hyperparameters fixed; 4 particles run 3 iterations, about 12 network evaluations, with no baseline (default setting, random search or other optimizer) and a single reported result, although the authors note that a metaheuristic needs repeated runs and statistical analysis. Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent} give the most complete comparison we found in the waste domain: a multi-objective beluga whale optimizer (MBWO) tunes the learning rate, batch size and dropout period of InceptionV3 on TrashNet (population 30, 20 iterations, 15 epochs per evaluation); four other metaheuristics share the population, iterations, bounds and fitness; an untuned model gives a reference (90.76\% accuracy); and a Wilcoxon rank-sum test on the best search fitness over independent runs finds MBWO significantly better than three competitors but not the fourth (EO). The number of runs is not stated, random search is absent, test metrics are single numbers without intervals, and the headline accuracy differs between the abstract (97.75\%) and the results table (97.57\%). Mao et al.~\cite{mao2021recycling}$^{\dagger}$ apply a genetic algorithm to a CNN that classifies recycling waste.

\textbf{Gap 1 (task and scene).} The classifiers of Section~\ref{subsec:rw-waste}~\cite{yang2016classification,kaya2023classification,sayed2024intelligent},~\cite{alshammari2025smart,kumsetty2022trashbox}$^{\dagger}$ assign one label per image, the TrashNet-based ones to one object on a plain background, and Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} find whole-image classification the most common approach; even some detection studies rest on near-single-object data~\cite{melinte2020deep,kumar2021novel,isalm2025ugvnbwaste}. The counter-evidence is substantial: multi-object waste detection is an established line of work, largely in computer-vision, robotics and sensing venues, with the datasets and pipeline of Section~\ref{subsec:rw-waste}~\cite{proenca2020taco,bashkirova2022zerowaste,cheng2021flow,yudin2024hierarchical}$^{\dagger}$,~\cite{kraft2021autonomous,cordova2022litter,hong2020trashcan,majchrowska2022deep}; as our review is not systematic, we do not estimate how much smaller that strand is. The gap is: \emph{in the literature we reviewed, waste recognition for sorting is dominated by single-label classification of one object on a plain background; multi-object detection in cluttered scenes is a smaller strand, and several detection studies still rely on single-object studio images.}

\textbf{Gap 2 (optimization).} Metaheuristic tuning in waste vision targets classifiers~\cite{sayed2024intelligent},~\cite{mao2021recycling}$^{\dagger}$, and PSO tuning of CNNs appears in other domains~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn}. No waste detection or segmentation model study read in full describes a search over training hyperparameters (Table~\ref{tab:lit-matrix}), the only described search being over post-processing parameters~\cite{wang2020multilevel}; in the sections we read, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2023artificial} mention PSO and genetic algorithms only for routing and prediction. Against this, metaheuristic CNN tuning does exist, so the gap concerns only its combination with waste detectors; two detection papers mention tuning without describing it~\cite{liao2022realtime,bartolo2025learning}, so a search there cannot be ruled out; and we could not check the training setup of detection works read only as abstracts. The gap is: \emph{in the literature we reviewed, no waste detector had its training hyperparameters tuned by a swarm optimizer; detection studies used default or hand-set values, or a tuning step they do not describe.}

\textbf{Gap 3 (evidence).} Where tuning is evaluated, baselines are weak: none in Aguerchi et al.~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn} and only other metaheuristics in Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent}, with single-number test metrics (Section~\ref{subsec:rw-hpo}). No detection or segmentation paper read in full reports run-to-run variation, confidence intervals or significance tests on its accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:lit-matrix}); Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning} state that statistical analysis was not applicable. Against this, Sayed et al. compare optimizers at equal population and iterations, with an untuned reference and a significance test on search fitness, so we do not claim that no study compares optimizers or tests significance. The gap is: \emph{metaheuristic tuning in this area is compared against other metaheuristics or against nothing; we found no comparison with random search~\cite{bergstra2012random} at an equal evaluation budget, and test results are reported as single numbers without confidence intervals.} SwarmSort narrows this gap without closing it: with one training seed per arm and one search seed per method, its intervals cover test-set sampling only (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

\textbf{Gap 4 (generalization).} The TrashNet-based classifiers never leave the studio, two reviews point to simplified, lab-like data~\cite{lu2022computer}$^{\dagger}$,~\cite{fang2023artificial}, and accuracy depends strongly on the dataset (the PlastOPol example of Section~\ref{sec:intro}~\cite{cordova2022litter}). Most detection studies test within one dataset or report new-site degradation only qualitatively (Table~\ref{tab:lit-matrix}), and the Indian work of Section~\ref{subsec:rw-india} offers no detection benchmark. Against this, cross-domain tests do exist: Bartolo et al.~\cite{bartolo2025learning}, in a recent workshop paper, test on datasets unseen in training, Fulton et al.~\cite{fulton2019robotic} on footage shifted in time and place, and S\'{a}nchez-Ferrer et al.~\cite{sanchezferrer2023experimental}$^{\dagger}$ add synthetic scenes to scarce real data; South Asian detection data exist in UGV-NBWASTE~\cite{isalm2025ugvnbwaste}, without baselines, and in the pLitterStreet preprint~\cite{mandhati2024plitterstreet}$^{\dagger}$. The gap is: \emph{studio-to-real-world and cross-dataset evaluation is rare and recent, and we found no Indian real-world waste test set with published object-detection baselines.} SwarmSort reports studio, real-world and DWSD results separately and compares studio-only with mixed training on the same test images (Section~\ref{subsec:res-domain}); its DWSD boxes come from mask regions that merge touching items, with a class mapping inferred from the masks, so the DWSD results also measure a labeling mismatch.

\textbf{Observation O5 (reporting of positive results).} The applied papers we read present their methods as improvements; Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent}, for example, call their optimizer the best despite its non-significant edge over EO. Partial negative findings do appear: a CNN far below an SVM~\cite{yang2016classification}, direct multi-class detection set aside as poor~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, synthetic data that relieve data scarcity only to a limited extent~\cite{sanchezferrer2023experimental}$^{\dagger}$, and weaker performance on shifted data~\cite{fulton2019robotic,liao2022realtime}. We found no paper reporting that a tuning method failed to beat a simple baseline; as evidence of absence from a targeted sample, this is an observation, not a gap. SwarmSort reports and analyzes its null and negative results (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:discussion}); Table~\ref{tab:gap-map} maps each gap to how SwarmSort addresses it.
