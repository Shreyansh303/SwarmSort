\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why PSO Did Not Beat Random Search}
\label{subsec:why}

In \genone, the defaults seem to sit in a broad, flat region of good settings. They beat most proxy evaluations of both searches and trailed the two winners by only 0.019 (PSO) and 0.023 (random search) (Section~\ref{subsec:res-search}); in the proxy check they ranked second of eight at both training lengths, and the four best settings lay within 0.026 after 40 epochs (Table~\ref{tab:proxy}).

Within that region, the proxy separated poor settings from good ones ($\rho = 0.976$) but could not pick a winner: its \genone winner, random search's setting, gave the lowest validation score after 100 epochs (Table~\ref{tab:gen1-test}). The short proxy may favor fast-learning settings, and the maximum of 32 single-run scores favors scores inflated by chance; we measured neither effect.

The budget was also small: 32 evaluations in six dimensions, eight of them spent by PSO on its random initial swarm. Bergstra and Bengio~\cite{bergstra2012random} report that for most data sets only a few hyperparameters really matter and that random search finds models as good as or better than grid search at the same budget. If one or two of our dimensions dominate, 32 independent samples cover them densely, leaving a guided search little to exploit; the below-default initial learning rate of all four search winners fits this view, though per-dimension importance was not measured.

The swarm converged early, raising its mean proxy score above that of random search but not its best (Section~\ref{subsec:res-search}). In \gentwo, its best was the last particle of the random initial swarm (Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}), so PSO in effect offered the best of eight random samples against the best of 32. With three update steps, a global-best swarm concentrates later evaluations around round~1's best point, raising the mean but not the maximum, the only score used.

Why tuning helped only in \gentwo is less clear. We hypothesize that the new data (real-world photographs, 640~px, a seventh class) moved the good region away from the defaults, so any reasonable search gained about 0.02 \mapfifty on the mixed and studio test sets; with no \gentwo proxy run of the defaults, this is untested. As each arm was trained once, part of the gain could also be training noise; repeating the proxy check and full trainings for \gentwo would tell the two apart (Section~\ref{subsec:fw-opt}).

\subsection{Implications for Tuning Claims in Waste AI}
\label{subsec:implications}

Gap~3 (Section~\ref{subsec:gaps}) is that metaheuristic tuning in this area is compared with other metaheuristics or with nothing, using single test numbers; our results show the cost. Against the defaults alone, PSO's significant \gentwo gain of $+0.020$ \mapfifty would have been credited to the swarm, yet random search gained $+0.017$ at the same budget and the tuned arms cannot be told apart (Table~\ref{tab:gen2-test}): the gain belongs to searching at all. Single numbers would also have misled: the \genone point estimates rank the defaults first and PSO last, and \gentwo validation ranked random search ahead of PSO, yet neither ordering is significant on test. Conversely, PSO's significant $+0.003$ over random search on India, on scores near 0.02, shows that a difference must also matter in practice.

We therefore recommend a minimum protocol for any claim that a metaheuristic tunes a waste model better: (1) include the defaults and random search at the same number of evaluations, and report time (Section~\ref{subsec:res-cost}); (2) check any cheap proxy against longer training before the search, with a threshold fixed in advance; (3) choose each arm's final setting on validation data and score the test split once; (4) report paired confidence intervals on arm differences per test domain, judged against a practically relevant difference; and (5) repeat trainings and searches with several seeds. SwarmSort meets all but the fifth. In equal-budget comparisons such as that of Sayed et al.~\cite{sayed2024intelligent}, a random-search arm would cost only one more competitor.

\subsection{Real-World and Indian Deployment}
\label{subsec:deployment}

For a smart bin or a municipal service, the data matter far more than the optimizer. Every arm loses about half its studio score on real-world photographs and collapses to 0.023--0.027 \mapfifty on Indian ones (Section~\ref{subsec:res-domain}); the largest tuning effect, about 0.02, is less than a tenth of the studio-to-real-world drop (about 0.28) and did not appear on real-world photographs. The strongest evidence is Table~\ref{tab:g1g2}, which scores both generations on the same images. Real-world training data raised real-world \mapfifty from 0.038--0.096 (\genone) to 0.338--0.353 (\gentwo), every \gentwo arm significantly above every \genone arm, whereas tuning made no significant real-world difference in either generation. The price was a significant studio loss of 0.074 to 0.105. Its three likely causes, which our runs do not separate, are that \gentwo kept only 41\% of the \genone studio training images, that the same small YOLOv8n shares its capacity with real-world scenes and a seventh class, and that \gentwo trained at 640 rather than 416~px and chose its checkpoint on mixed validation data. Local training data will therefore help more than a better search, which is why Indian data lead the roadmap (Section~\ref{subsec:fw-data}). Clutter is the second obstacle: the models label every object correctly on about 44\% of the single-object real-world photographs but on none of the 46 with three or more objects (Fig.~\ref{fig:best}), so a bin that photographs one item at a time is a realistic target today, while sorting a mixed pile is not.

Because DWSD's merged, mask-derived boxes penalize correct per-item detections (Section~\ref{subsec:res-domain}), a test set must be labeled like the training data before its score can be read as accuracy; relabeling part of DWSD item by item would separate labeling style from model error.

Indian rules also constrain the classes: Rule~15(h) of the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016~\cite{moefcc2016solid} requires green, white and black bins for bio-degradable, recyclable and other waste. BIODEGRADABLE maps to green and the material classes, insofar as their items are recyclable, to white, but black corresponds to OTHER, every arm's weakest class (AP@50 0.050 to 0.063), where a bin built on these models would be least reliable.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{subsec:limitations}

Each arm was trained once (seed 42) and each search run once (PSO seed 42, random-search seed 123), so the intervals cover test sampling only, and another seed could change the small arm differences. No $p$-values or multiple-comparison corrections were computed for the twelve pairwise comparisons per metric in \gentwo (three pairs, four test sets), per-class AP has no interval, and the real-world test set's single BIODEGRADABLE box makes its intervals wide and lopsided.

The proxy check used eight settings, \genone only and a 40-epoch rather than a 100-epoch reference, so proxy validity in \gentwo is assumed. The proxy scored last-epoch weights, whereas the final models used the best validation checkpoint (\texttt{best.pt}). The \gentwo Arm~C training, resumed after epoch 55 when a session froze, kept the same weights and optimizer state but is not bit-identical to an uninterrupted run.

DWSD's class mapping was inferred from the masks, with six of the fifteen mask values uncertain, and its boxes are connected regions rather than single items. Only six training hyperparameters were searched, and the budget was equal in evaluations, not in time. No external baseline, such as the pipeline of Majchrowska et al.~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, was run on our data, so our scores cannot be ranked against prior work. Finally, the literature review was targeted rather than systematic (Section~\ref{subsec:review-method}), so the gaps (Section~\ref{subsec:gaps}) hold only for the literature we reviewed.
