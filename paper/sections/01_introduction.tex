\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{W}{aste} can be recycled, composted or safely disposed of only if its materials are kept apart. In India, the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 require every waste generator to segregate waste at source into bio-degradable, non-bio-degradable and domestic hazardous streams (Rule~4), with green bins for bio-degradable, white bins for recyclable and black bins for other waste (Rule~15(h))~\cite{moefcc2016solid}. Such segregation depends on many people sorting many items correctly, and a camera that names the material of each item could support it at a smart bin, on a sorting line or at a collection point. Artificial intelligence (AI) is already an established tool in solid waste management: a systematic review covers 85 studies from 2004 to 2019~\cite{abdallah2020artificial}, and a later review of AI for waste management in smart cities covers smart bins, sorting robots and image-based waste identification~\cite{fang2023artificial}.

In the literature we reviewed, however, most vision work on waste sorting solves an easier problem than a real bin or street presents. Smart-bin and recycling studies usually classify images: one label for one photograph of one object, often on a plain background, as in the TrashNet images of Yang and Thung~\cite{yang2016classification} and the classifiers trained on them~\cite{kaya2023classification}. A bin with a single-item chute can work this way, but a heap, a conveyor or a street scene holds many items of different sizes, some touching or partly hidden, and each must be located as well as named. That is an object-detection task. Majchrowska et al.~\cite{majchrowska2022deep}, the starting point of this work, detect waste in natural and urban environments, localizing litter with one network and classifying each detected crop with another. Multi-object waste detection is thus established, but it is a smaller strand than single-object classification, and several detection studies still rely on single-object studio images: the task and scene gap (G1).

Detectors are also sensitive to how they are trained. Training hyperparameters such as the learning rate, momentum, weight decay and augmentation strength can decide whether a network learns at all: Yang and Thung attributed the 22\% test accuracy of their convolutional neural network (CNN), against 63\% for a support vector machine, to unsuitable hyperparameters~\cite{yang2016classification}. Metaheuristic optimizers have been used to choose such values for CNNs. Particle swarm optimization (PSO)~\cite{kennedy1995particle} selected the architecture hyperparameters of a mammography classifier~\cite{aguerchi2024cnn}, and a multi-objective beluga whale optimizer tuned an InceptionV3 waste classifier~\cite{sayed2024intelligent}. The evidence that such tuning helps is weaker than the reported gains suggest. In the studies we reviewed, metaheuristic tuning in waste vision targeted classifiers, and no waste detector had its training hyperparameters tuned by a swarm optimizer (G2). Tuned models were compared with other metaheuristics or with nothing, not with random search~\cite{bergstra2012random} at the same number of evaluations, and test results were single numbers without confidence intervals (G3). Without such a baseline, a gain cannot be credited to the optimizer's guidance rather than to the search effort itself. Section~\ref{sec:related} gives the evidence for both gaps.

A second weakness concerns the data behind the results. Reviews find many computer-vision studies of waste sorting set in simplified environments and built on artificially collected data~\cite{lu2022computer}, and note that simple lab pictures differ from real waste~\cite{fang2023artificial}. Accuracy also depends heavily on the dataset: in one comparative study, the same YOLOv5x detector, trained and tested on each dataset separately, scored 84.9 AP$_{50}$ on the PlastOPol litter dataset but 63.3 on TACO~\cite{cordova2022litter}. A tuning gain measured on studio images may therefore say little about street photographs. For India, where segregation at source is mandatory, the evidence is thinner still: the closest resource, the Dense Waste Segmentation Dataset (DWSD) from West Bengal~\cite{ali2025dwsd}, reports segmentation pixel accuracy only, and we found no Indian real-world waste test set with published object-detection baselines (G4).

SwarmSort tests the tuning question under strict conditions. It trains YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023ultralytics}, a nano single-stage detector small enough for a smart bin or a phone, and compares three ways, called arms, of choosing six of its training hyperparameters: the library defaults (Arm~A), the best of 32 random samples (Arm~B) and the best setting found by a hand-written PSO with 8 particles over 4 rounds (Arm~C). Both searches thus spend 32 proxy trainings of 12 epochs on a 40\% subset of the training data, and a check with a pass threshold fixed in advance tested whether this proxy ranks settings like longer training. Each arm's chosen setting is then trained once for 100 epochs and tested once on a held-out split, with paired-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals on every difference. The study ran in two generations: \genone used studio-style images in six classes, and \gentwo added real-world photographs, a seventh class (OTHER) and a test-only Indian set built from DWSD. We address three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1:] At an equal budget of 32 proxy trainings, does PSO find YOLOv8n training hyperparameters that beat random search and the default settings on a held-out test set? (G2, G3)
\item[RQ2:] Does a short proxy training (12 epochs on 40\% of the data) rank hyperparameter settings the same way longer training does? (G3)
\item[RQ3:] Does the answer to RQ1 change when the data move from studio images to real-world and Indian scenes, and how large is the studio-to-real-world drop? (G1, G4)
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions, each tied to a gap or to the reporting of results, are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{A lightweight multi-object waste detector} (G1): YOLOv8n trained on multi-object studio and real-world scenes, with six classes in \genone and seven in \gentwo (Sections~\ref{subsec:datasets} and~\ref{subsec:detector}).
\item \textit{A swarm-tuned waste detector} (G2): a hand-written PSO searches six training hyperparameters of the detector, covering the learning-rate schedule, momentum, weight decay and two augmentation settings (Sections~\ref{subsec:space} and~\ref{subsec:pso}). To our knowledge, within the literature we reviewed, no earlier waste detector had its training hyperparameters tuned by a swarm optimizer.
\item \textit{An equal-budget, three-arm protocol} (G3): defaults, random search and PSO at 32 proxy trainings each; a proxy-validity check with a pass threshold fixed in advance, which the proxy passed with Spearman $\rho = 0.976$ ($n = 8$, \genone only); a test split used once; and paired-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals on every difference (Sections~\ref{subsec:design}, \ref{subsec:proxy} and~\ref{subsec:eval}).
\item \textit{A two-generation evaluation across domains} (G4): studio, real-world and Indian results reported separately, including object-detection results on DWSD, for which, to our knowledge, within the literature we reviewed, no detection baselines had been published (Section~\ref{subsec:res-domain}). Our DWSD boxes come from mask regions that merge touching items, and the class mapping was inferred from the masks, so these results also measure a labeling mismatch.
\item \textit{An honest answer, with null results reported} (O5): PSO never meaningfully beat random search at an equal budget; its only significant edge, $+0.003$ \mapfifty on the Indian set, lies on scores near zero. Tuning gains over the defaults were absent on the studio data of \genone, small in \gentwo ($+0.017$ for random search and $+0.020$ for PSO on the mixed test set) and did not reach real-world or Indian photographs. We explain these results (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) and release the code, search logs, result files and trained models.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the related work and derives gaps G1--G4. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the design, data, detector, search methods, proxy fitness and evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} answers RQ2, RQ1 and RQ3, in that order. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} explains why PSO did not beat random search, draws implications for tuning studies and for deployment in India, and states the limitations (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}). Section~\ref{sec:future} turns them into a prioritized roadmap of improvements, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
